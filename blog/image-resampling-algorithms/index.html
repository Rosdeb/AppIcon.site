Resampling Algorithms Explained: Nearest Neighbor vs Bilinear vs Bicubic

The mathematics of pixel scaling: understand how Nearest Neighbor, Bilinear, Bicubic, and Lanczos interpolation calculate new pixel values during scaling.

Published November 2026 • 8 min read • App Icon Studio Engineering Team

⚡ Key Takeaways

  • Resampling calculates new pixel colors when scaling an image beyond or below its native resolution grid.
  • Nearest Neighbor retains razor-sharp hard edges without blurring, making it ideal for retro pixel art and QR codes.
  • Bilinear interpolation averages adjacent 2x2 pixel clusters, offering fast general-purpose performance.
  • Bicubic interpolation evaluates 4x4 pixel matrices with cubic spline curves, delivering smooth photographic gradients and sharp edges.

What Happens When You Resize an Image?

When you resize a raster image from 2000×2000px down to 500×500px (downsampling), you discard 15 out of every 16 pixels. Conversely, when you upscale an image from 100×100px to 400×400px, your computer must invent 15 new pixels for every original pixel that existed. The mathematical rules used to compute these new values are called interpolation or resampling algorithms.

The Three Core Resampling Philosophies

1. Nearest Neighbor: The simplest interpolation algorithm. It assigns each new coordinate the exact color of the closest existing pixel. There is zero blur, zero color blending, and instant calculation speed. However, diagonal lines show heavy jagged staircasing. This is the preferred algorithm for retro 8-bit game pixel art and QR codes where strict color purity must be preserved.

2. Bilinear Interpolation: Computes the weighted average of the four nearest neighboring pixels (a 2×2 grid) based on proximity. It softens jagged edges, but can cause a noticeable hazy blur across sharp contrast boundaries.

3. Bicubic Interpolation: Evaluates the sixteen closest pixels (a 4×4 grid) using cubic spline polynomial curves. By considering rate-of-change and edge gradients, it produces vastly sharper, cleaner results than bilinear interpolation, making it the industry standard for photographic resizing.

Avoiding Moiré Patterns During Heavy Downsampling

When downsampling images containing fine repetitive textures (e.g., striped clothing, architectural brickwork, or dense screen grids), simple decimation causes aliasing and swirling interference patterns known as Moiré.

Modern browser Canvas implementations apply anti-aliased multi-step downsampling passes to suppress high-frequency noise before final pixel rasterization. App Icon Studio's Image Resizer incorporates optimized interpolation passes so downscaled assets remain crystal clear.
